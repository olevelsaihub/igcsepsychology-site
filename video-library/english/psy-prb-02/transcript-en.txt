Idea. Does depression run in families? Genes are instructions you inherit from your parents. The genetic explanation says some people inherit a higher risk of depression. This is a nature explanation, which means it is inherited. Caspi and colleagues tested it in two thousand and three, using the five H T T gene. Caspi's study was longitudinal, which means it followed the same people for years. It followed children from New Zealand from age three. At twenty-six, eight hundred and forty-seven were used. Stressful life events between twenty-one and twenty-six were counted. Depression was checked in an interview. Two numbers to remember. About fifteen per cent of people had four or more stressful life events. And seventeen per cent had a major depressive episode in the past year. The key finding. The three gene groups had similar numbers of stressful events. But people with one or two short alleles were much more likely to be depressed after many stressful events. Genes and the environment worked together. This is a gene-environment interaction. A question describes a person and asks how likely they are to be depressed, or how genes contributed. You use Caspi's finding, and the clues in the person.

Method. Step one: decide whether the question wants recall of the study, or application to a person. Step two: state the finding, a short allele plus many stressful events raises the risk. Step three: point to the family clue and the stressful events in the scenario. The traps are saying someone has the depression gene, as if genes alone cause it, and mixing up which allele raises the risk. It is the short allele. And do not misquote the numbers.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Bob's father had depression and has died. Bob lost his job, and one of his children has had cancer twice. We explain how likely Bob is to develop depression, using Caspi. Two marks. Step one: state the finding. People with at least one short five H T T allele, who have many stressful life events, are more likely to become depressed. Step two: find the clues in Bob. His father had depression, so Bob may have inherited a short allele. Then the stressful events: a child with cancer twice, his father's death, and losing his job. A tempting wrong route is to say Bob has the depression gene, so he will definitely become depressed. Caspi found genes alone do not decide. Say Bob is more likely, not certain. The exam answer. Finding first, then Bob. One mark for stating the finding accurately. One mark for applying it to Bob's inherited risk and stressful events. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is mixing up the alleles. The risk comes from the short allele, not the long one. Two long alleles carried the lowest risk.

Your turn. Now you try. This time the problem is addiction, not depression, but genes work in the same way. Explain why Olivia may have developed her addiction. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Genes are passed from parents to children. Olivia's mother and father were both addicted to the same drug. So Olivia may have inherited genes that make addiction more likely. One example is a version of the D R D two gene, which affects dopamine, a brain chemical linked to reward. This could explain why she finds it hard to stop. A common wrong answer says Olivia copied her parents. That is learning from the environment, not genes. The question asks about the influence of genes, so it loses the marks. So state the genetic idea, find the inherited clue in the person, and say they are more likely, not certain.
