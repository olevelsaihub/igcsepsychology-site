Idea. Does watching violent films make children violent? One way to find out is to wait for television to arrive somewhere that never had it. Charlton and colleagues, two thousand, studied children on St Helena, a remote island. Television came in nineteen ninety-five. It was a natural experiment, because the researchers did not control it. TV simply arrived. They used observation. They video recorded children playing in school playgrounds, before and after TV arrived. The children were chosen randomly. Behaviour was coded as pro-social or antisocial. The findings were surprising. There was little change in behaviour after TV arrived. Boys showed far more antisocial acts than girls, but this was not linked to TV. The close community may have protected the children. A question gives a person in a similar setting and asks how Charlton's findings explain their behaviour. You pick a finding that fits.

Method. Step one: pick the study detail that fits. That could be community supervision, the gender difference, no TV effect, or the procedure. Step two: point to the scenario detail that matches, such as a small village where everyone knows everyone. Step three: justify with that finding from the study. The trap is saying Charlton found TV made children aggressive. He did not.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Simone lives in a small village where everyone knows everyone. A cinema opens. Simone shows no change, but Paul gets into trouble at college. We must explain two ways Charlton's study accounts for this. Way one is Simone. Her village is small and close, so adults watch what she does. Charlton found that the close, watchful community helped explain why children did not become more antisocial after TV arrived. Way two is Paul. His name suggests he is a boy. Charlton found boys carried out far more antisocial acts than girls, and this was there with or without TV. So Paul's trouble may be because he is male, not because of the films. A tempting wrong route is to say the films made Paul violent. Reject it. Charlton did not find that TV caused aggression. Now write it out, one way at a time. Each way earns a point and a justification. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is to say Charlton found TV caused aggression. In fact, behaviour hardly changed, and the study points to the close community. Do not mix it up with Bandura's study of copying in a laboratory.

Answer. Bella lives in a small village, so the community is close and adults supervise what she does. Charlton found that a close community on St Helena helped children stay pro-social after TV arrived. So Bella's violent films have not changed her behaviour.
