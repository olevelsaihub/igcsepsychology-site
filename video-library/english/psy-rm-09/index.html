Idea. Ask your class two things. How many hours did you sleep? And how did you feel this morning? The first answer is a number. The second answer is words. Number data is called quantitative data. Data in words is called qualitative data. Closed questions, such as tick one box, give quantitative data. So does counting. Open questions, where people answer in their own words, give qualitative data. So do diaries. Now think about who collected the data. If you ran the survey yourself, that is primary data. It is first-hand. If you use data someone else already collected, such as government statistics or a newspaper article, that is secondary data. These are two separate questions. One is about what the data looks like. The other is about who collected it. This question type asks you to name a type of data, define it, or give an example from the study.

Method. Step one: are the data numbers or words? That decides quantitative or qualitative. Step two: did the researcher collect it? That decides primary or secondary. Step three: if asked, add an example taken from the study. The trap is calling a rating scale qualitative because it is about feelings. A rating from one to ten is a number. So it is quantitative, whatever the topic.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Davide studies how a school ban on unhealthy food changes the attitudes of young people. He plans further research that gathers qualitative data. We must define qualitative data and give an example. It is worth two marks. Step one. Define it. Qualitative data is information in words, images or meanings, rather than numbers. Step two. The example must fit Davide. He could ask pupils an open question, such as: how do you feel about the ban? They answer in their own words, and he records what they say. A tempting wrong example is the rating from one to ten that Davide already used. That gives numbers, so it is quantitative. It would not earn the mark. Now the exam-ready answer. Qualitative data is descriptive information in words, images or meanings rather than numbers. For example, Davide could ask pupils the open question, how do you feel about the ban on unhealthy food, and record what they say. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to call a researcher's own recordings secondary data. Secondary data already existed. Someone else collected it earlier. If the researcher collected it for this study, it is primary.

Your turn. Now you try. Rocio is studying one patient with an alcohol addiction. She recorded the units of alcohol the patient drank each month. Describe what quantitative data means, using an example from her case study. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Quantitative data is data in numbers. Here the example is the units of alcohol. In March the patient drank two hundred and ninety eight units. That is a number, so it is quantitative. A common wrong answer is to point to the recorded conversations. Those are words, so they are qualitative. The table of units is the quantitative part. Choosing the conversations loses the example mark. So decide numbers or words. Decide who collected it. Then give an example from the study.
