Idea. You look at a cloud. A friend says, that looks like a dragon. Suddenly you cannot stop seeing a dragon. One word changed what you saw. This is what Carmichael, Hogan and Walter tested in nineteen thirty-two. A verbal label is a word that names something. Their aim was to see whether a label heard while looking at a picture changes how it is later remembered and drawn. They showed twelve ambiguous drawings one at a time. An ambiguous drawing can be seen in more than one way. Each drawing was given one of two names, such as eyeglasses or dumbbells. A control group heard no names. Then everyone redrew the figures from memory. About three quarters of drawings in the named groups looked more like the named object. In the control group fewer than half did. So words heard at the time of seeing change how a shape is perceived and drawn. The control group makes comparison easy. But the task is artificial, and the label may change memory, not perception. Questions ask how this study accounts for a scenario.

Method. Step one: say how the label changed what the person saw or drew. Step two: justify with the study, where labelled drawings looked more like the named object. The trap is saying the label changed the picture. It changed the person's perception. Nine mark questions compare the scenario with the original study. Describe the study, match the groups, evaluate the similarities and differences, then judge how far the study explains it.

Worked example. Here is a real four mark question. Helga and Lena drew maps. The warden told Helga to show canoes. Helga's boats looked like canoes. Lena's looked like the real boats. We explain two ways the findings account for this. First, match each person to a group in the study. Helga heard a label, canoes, so she is like the named groups. Lena heard none, so she is like the control group. Way one is Helga. The word canoes changed how she perceived and drew the boats. In the study, about three quarters of the named groups' drawings looked more like the named object. So her boats looked like canoes. Way two is Lena. She heard no label, like the control group. In the study, fewer than half of the control drawings looked like either name. So her boats stayed close to the real boats. A tempting wrong route is to say the warden changed the real boats. The label changes only how the person perceives and draws it. Helga was told about canoes, so, as in the study's named groups, her drawing looked more like canoes. Lena heard no word, like the control group, so her boats stayed realistic. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The classic slip is saying the label changed the original picture. The picture never changes. The label changes what the person perceives, remembers or draws.

Your turn. Alena used Rose's map, took wrong turns, and thought a drawing of a pond was a roundabout. Explain two ways the findings of Carmichael, Hogan and Walter could account for this. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Way one: the pond drawing is ambiguous, like the study's figures, where one shape could be eyeglasses or dumbbells. So a round pond could be read as a roundabout. Way two: no word label said pond. In the study, labels pulled figures towards the named object. So Alena gave it her own label, roundabout, as she was looking for roads. The common wrong answer says Alena was careless or bad at maps. That uses no study, so it earns no marks. So say how the label changed perception. Then justify with the finding.
