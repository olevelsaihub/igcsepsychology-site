Visual agnosia, prosopagnosia and pre-frontal damage

Read the narration

Idea. You see a friend across the road. You know who it is at once, from the face. Now imagine seeing that same friend and having no idea who they are. Your eyes work perfectly. The problem is recognising. That is what some brain injuries do. Visual agnosia is when a person can see normally but cannot recognise or name objects. They may be able to copy the shape of a key, but not say it is a key. They may name it by touch instead. Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, is when a person cannot recognise familiar faces, even of family, but can still recognise objects. Prosopagnosia is often noticed in daily life. People may rely on voice, clothes or hair to know who someone is. They may struggle to follow a film, or to recognise themselves in a photo. Neither condition means blindness. A third area is the pre-frontal cortex. This is the front part of the brain, behind the forehead. Damage here can cause poor decisions, impulsive behaviour, flat emotions and a change in personality. So this question type gives you a person with a brain injury. You must decide which condition fits, define it, and link it to the evidence.

Method. Step one: look at what the person can and cannot recognise. Objects not recognised means visual agnosia. Faces not recognised means prosopagnosia. Step two: define the condition accurately. That is the first mark. Step three: link it to the evidence in the scenario. That is the second mark. The trap is mixing the two names up, or saying the person is blind. Check the evidence before choosing the name, and say eyesight is normal.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Matthew tests a patient with a brain injury. The patient is shown keys, a games console and an anchor. They cannot name them. They are then shown a photo of someone, and they do recognise the person. We explain what the results indicate, using a concept of neurological damage. Step one. Read the evidence. The objects failed. The face did not. Step two. Objects not recognised, with a face recognised, means visual agnosia. Define it: the person can see normally but cannot recognise what they are looking at. A tempting wrong route is prosopagnosia, because it sounds like the recognition problem. But prosopagnosia is about faces, and this patient recognised the face. So it does not fit. Now the exam-ready answer. The results suggest visual agnosia, where someone's eyesight works but they cannot recognise what they are looking at. The patient could not name the keys, console or anchor, but did recognise the person in the photo, so the problem is with objects, not faces. That is one mark for the definition and one for the link to the results. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say people with agnosia are blind. They are not. Their eyes work. The damage is to the part of the brain that recognises.

Your turn. Now you try. Neha is an artist who cannot recognise the faces of people she knows. She has a diagnosed condition. Explain one way she will be affected. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Faces are the problem, so the condition is prosopagnosia. This is when a person cannot recognise familiar faces. So Neha may not recognise the faces of her students or her friends, and may need to use their voices or clothes instead. A common wrong answer says Neha cannot see faces, or is going blind. Prosopagnosia does not damage eyesight. She sees the face. She cannot recognise whose it is. So that answer loses the mark. So ask what cannot be recognised. Name the condition and define it. Then link it to the person.
