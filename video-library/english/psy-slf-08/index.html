Idea. Do pets help children grow up? Many parents think so. In nineteen ninety-five, Van Houtte and Jarvis tested the idea. They studied one hundred and thirty children in the USA, in grades three to eight. Only a cat or a dog counted as a pet. Owners were compared with non-owners who were matched on things like family background. The children filled in questionnaires. They measured self-esteem, which is how much you value yourself. They measured autonomy, which is being able to decide and act for yourself. They also measured self-concept and life satisfaction. The findings were these. Pet owners scored higher on autonomy. Among fifth and sixth graders, owners also had higher self-esteem. The benefit came from having a pet, not from how attached the child was. Now evaluate. The groups were matched, and pets are used in therapy, which supports the idea. But the data was self-report, only cats and dogs counted, and this kind of comparison cannot prove that pets cause the change. A question gives you a child and asks how the study can explain their behaviour. You choose the finding that fits, then link it to the child.

Method. Step one: pick the finding that fits. Is the child independent, or is it about confidence? Autonomy and self-esteem are different findings. Step two: link it to the child, using their age and what they do. If the question says explain two ways, use two different findings, one for each point. Each way needs the finding and the link to the child. The trap is the age rule. The self-esteem finding is for fifth and sixth graders only. Autonomy is for pet owners overall.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Mable and Kelsey are both eleven. Mable has a dog and feeds it. She is confident alone and in groups. Kelsey has no pet. He is less confident and wants other children around. We explain two ways the findings could account for this. Four marks. Way one: Mable owns a dog, which counts as a pet. Pet owners had higher autonomy. She looks after the dog herself, so she is happy to work alone. Way two: Kelsey is eleven, so in the fifth or sixth grade age group. Owners there had higher self-esteem. Kelsey has no pet, so lower self-esteem fits his worry about being liked. A tempting wrong route is to say having no pet makes Kelsey unconfident. Reject it. The study cannot prove a pet causes the change. Say the findings fit, not that they prove it. Now write it out. Each way has a finding and a link. Two marks for each way. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say every age group had higher self-esteem. Only fifth and sixth graders did. The higher autonomy was the finding for owners overall.

Your turn. Now you try. Jessica feels stressed and visits a counsellor. The only time she feels relaxed is walking her neighbour's dog. Explain one suggestion the counsellor could give her to cope, referring to a study. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. The counsellor could suggest that Jessica gets a dog of her own, or walks a dog more often than once a month. Van Houtte and Jarvis found that pet owners had higher autonomy. Caring for a pet may help her feel more in control of her life, so she copes better with stress. The common wrong answer says dogs are relaxing, with no study. That gives a suggestion but no study, so it loses the mark. So pick the finding that fits, then link it to the person. Do it once for each way asked.
