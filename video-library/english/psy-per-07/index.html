Idea. A friend asks how tall a tree is. You look at a door nearby and think, the tree is about four doors high. You used something you already know to judge size. This is what Haber and Levin studied in two thousand and one. Their aim was to test whether judging an object's size and judging its distance are independent, which means separate processes. The sample was one hundred and nine male undergraduate students, who are university students. Objects were placed in three of the four quadrants of the field, at different distances. There were three kinds. Standard size objects always have the same size. Token variant objects are familiar but come in many sizes, such as a Christmas tree. The third kind was unfamiliar shapes. Distance estimates were accurate for every kind of object. Size estimates were accurate for standard size objects. But they were less accurate for token variant and unfamiliar objects. So we judge size mainly from memory of familiar objects, separately from distance. The question describes someone judging size or distance. You explain it using the finding for that kind of object.

Method. Step one: decide if the object is standard size, token variant or unfamiliar. Step two: say what the person got right or wrong. Step three: justify with the finding for that kind of object. The trap is saying distance judgements were poor. They were accurate. For strength and weakness questions, give one of each, and justify each. Weaknesses include the all male student sample and the artificial field setting.

Worked example. Here is a real two mark question. Colin buys curtains for his window. He estimated the window size, but it was larger than he thought. We explain why it was hard, using Haber and Levin. First classify the object. Windows come in many sizes. So a window is a token variant object. Mark one is the reason in context. Windows are not a fixed size, so Colin had no reliable remembered size to compare with. So he underestimated it. Mark two is the justification. In Haber and Levin's study, size estimates for token variant objects were less accurate than for standard size objects. Memory of the category does not give the real size. A tempting wrong route is to say Colin could not judge how far away the window was. But the study found distance estimates were accurate, so that loses the mark. Here is the answer. Windows come in many sizes, so Colin had no reliable remembered size to compare with. In Haber and Levin's study, size estimates of token variant objects were less accurate. So he misjudged the window. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The classic slip is saying distance judgements were poor. In the study, distance estimates were accurate for all objects. Only size was less accurate for some objects.

Your turn. Now you try. Killian sees a hotel far away. The cars outside are one point five metres tall and the hotel looks fifty times bigger, so he guesses seventy-five metres. It is fifty. Explain one strength and one weakness of using Haber and Levin. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Strength: a hotel is a token variant object, because hotels come in many sizes. Haber and Levin found size estimates for these were less accurate, which explains why Killian was twenty-five metres out. Weakness: the study took place in an open field. Killian judged the height of a tall building in a city, so the findings may not apply. A common wrong answer gives a weakness that does not fit Killian, such as the all male sample.
