Idea. Imagine two classrooms joined by one corridor. If the corridor is blocked, a message in one room cannot reach the other. Your brain is built like this. It has two halves, and each half is called a hemisphere. The corridor between them is the corpus callosum. Some people have severe epilepsy, which means sudden attacks of abnormal brain activity. To stop the attacks, surgeons once cut the corpus callosum. These people are called split-brain patients. Their two halves could no longer pass messages. The wiring crosses over. The left hand and the left side of what you see go to the right hemisphere. The right hand and the right side of vision go to the left hemisphere. The left hemisphere handles language, so it can speak. The right hemisphere cannot. Now the study. Sperry, nineteen sixty-eight, tested eleven split-brain patients. In one task they felt an object with one hand, out of sight. In another, a picture was flashed to one side of their vision. Each time, only one hemisphere received the information. A question describes a split-brain patient doing a task. You must say what the person can say or do, using Sperry.

Method. Step one: work out which hemisphere gets the information. Left hand or left vision means the right hemisphere. This comes first because everything else depends on it. Step two: recall what Sperry found for that side. Step three: say what the person can or cannot say or do, using details from the question. The trap is saying the patient cannot feel or find the object. They can find it. They just cannot name it. So write about speaking, not sensing.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Hina is a split-brain patient. She picks a marble from a bag using her left hand, without seeing it. She is then asked to tell the audience what she picked. We must explain what she is likely to say, using Sperry. It is worth two marks. Step one. Hina used her left hand. The left hand connects to the right hemisphere. So the marble is felt by the right hemisphere only. Step two. Sperry found that patients could not name objects felt with the left hand. The right hemisphere cannot speak, and the cut corpus callosum stops the information reaching the left hemisphere, which can. A tempting wrong route is to say Hina cannot tell what is in her hand because she cannot feel it. But she did feel it and pick it. Her problem is saying the word. Now the exam-ready answer. Sperry found that split-brain patients could not name objects felt with the left hand. So Hina probably cannot say marble, though she could point to it or pick it again. That is one mark for the finding and one for the application. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say the patient cannot find or sense the object. Sperry showed they can still pick it out. What they cannot do is say its name.
