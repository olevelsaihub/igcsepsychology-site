Idea. A friend says, I joined the team because everyone else did, and I learned the plays by practising. An area of psychology is a topic you have studied, such as memory or social influence. This question asks you to explain one person's behaviour using two areas. Useful areas include conformity, memory, obedience and development. Pick areas that clearly match what the person does, and have a study ready for each. The question says, assess the person's behaviour using two areas of psychology. Assess means weigh up how well the ideas explain it, and reach a judgement. Nine marks: three for knowledge, three for applying it to the person, and three for evaluating. Use only one area, and you cannot go above Level two.

Method. Step one: underline two or three behaviours that match different areas. Step two: explain each area accurately. Step three: apply each to details of the person, using their name. Step four: evaluate each with a named study and a limit. Step five: finish with a short judgement. The traps are using only one area, describing theories without linking them to the person, and forgetting a final judgement.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question, in two parts. Giovanni's friends all have part-time jobs, so he gets one delivering newspapers. He must remember a route. Later he sees an elderly person in distress, and rings the emergency services. We assess his behaviour, using two areas. Step one, the plan. Taking the job because friends had one is conformity. Learning the route is memory. Two clear areas are enough. Conformity means changing what you do to match the majority. Giovanni heard that most friends had jobs, so he got one, so he was not left out of the concert. Evaluate. Asch found about a third of critical answers conformed, and about three quarters conformed at least once. But his task was trivial, with American male students. Giovanni may also have wanted a ticket. Memory, in short. In the multi-store model, rehearsal, which means repeating, moves information into long-term memory. Giovanni repeats his route daily. The Petersons, in nineteen fifty-nine, stopped rehearsal by making people count backwards, and recall fell to about ten per cent after eighteen seconds. Finally, the judgement. Conformity explains why he took the job. Memory explains how he learned the route. But both rest on artificial laboratory studies, so each is only part of the explanation. Common mistake. A common mistake is writing at length about only one area. That caps the answer at Level two, six marks, however good it is.

Your turn. Now you try, with Petra's story. You do not need to write the whole essay: plan it. For each of two areas, note the idea, a detail, a study and a limit. Then add a judgement. Write your plan, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Area one is obedience. Her mother is an authority figure who gives a direct order, so Petra strokes the dog despite her fear. Milgram found sixty-five per cent obeyed all the way to four hundred and fifty volts. Limit: those were adult men in a laboratory. Area two is development. In Piaget's theory, a schema is a mental framework built from experience. The dog is a pet, like their cat, so Petra can fit it into her pet schema. That is assimilation. The three mountains task supports Piaget, but he may have underestimated young children. Judgement: obedience explains why she strokes the dog, and her schema explains why her fear can fall. Each explains only part of her behaviour. A common wrong answer just retells the story, with no psychology. Name the area, then use the story as the detail. So match two areas, explain, apply, evaluate, and judge.
