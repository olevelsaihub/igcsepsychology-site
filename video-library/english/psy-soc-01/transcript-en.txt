Idea. Your teacher says, put your phone away, and you do. That is obedience. Obedience means carrying out a direct order from someone you see as having legitimate authority, which means the right to give orders. Blind obedience is obeying without question, even when the order is wrong. In nineteen sixty-three, Milgram tested this. Forty American men played the teacher. They were told to give electric shocks to a learner, who was an actor, when he got answers wrong. Sixty-five per cent went all the way to four hundred and fifty volts. Every one went to at least three hundred volts. Milgram then changed the situation. When the experimenter gave orders by telephone, obedience fell. When two other teachers refused, only ten per cent went to the end. When someone else pressed the switch, obedience rose to ninety-two point five per cent. So how can we prevent blind obedience? Four ways. Disobedient role models, which means people who refuse. Distance from the authority. Personal responsibility, being reminded you alone are accountable. And education about obedience research. Questions often ask for two ways, with evidence.

Method. Step one: underline the authority figure and the order. Step two: name one way in a short sentence. Step three: justify it with Milgram and the result. Step four: link it to the people in the question. For two ways, use two different ideas. The traps are describing conformity, which is following a group, not an order. And giving a way with no study to justify it.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. A football manager orders his players to hurt the other team. We give two ways to prevent blind obedience. Four marks. Step one: the authority figure is the manager. The order is to hurt the other team. Every point must link back to them. Way one. Some players openly refuse, giving the rest disobedient role models. Milgram found that when two other teachers refused, only ten per cent went to the end, compared with sixty-five per cent in the original. So teammates refusing makes the rest less likely to obey. Way two. Remind the players that they alone will be punished, so they cannot blame the manager. Milgram found obedience rose to ninety-two point five per cent when someone else pressed the switch. So feeling fully responsible should lower blind obedience. A tempting wrong route is, the players should just refuse. That is common sense. The question says you must use research, so add Milgram's result. The exam answer. One mark for each way, and one for each piece of evidence. Common mistake. A common mistake is to quote the wrong variation. Two peers refusing gave ten per cent. Someone else pressing the switch gave ninety-two point five per cent. Mix them up and the evidence says the opposite.
