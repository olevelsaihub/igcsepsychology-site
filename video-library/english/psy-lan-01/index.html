Idea. A baby cannot say the word hungry, but she can reach for food. So which comes first, thinking or talking? In nineteen fifty, Piaget said thinking develops first, and language follows. A child can only name what they already understand. Two Piaget terms. A schema is a mental package of knowledge. With no schema for an object, a child cannot name it. Representational thinking means letting words stand for real objects and events. Piaget says this must come before language. In nineteen eighty-one, Vygotsky disagreed. He said thought and language start as two separate systems. They join up at around two to three years old. Before they join, there are two terms. Pre-linguistic thought is a baby thinking and solving problems without words, like pointing to get something. Pre-intellectual language is sounds or words with no thought behind them, like babbling. A question describes a baby or toddler, names Piaget or Vygotsky, and asks you to explain what the child does, using the right idea.

Method. Step one: decide which theory the question names. Step two: quote the child's behaviour from the stem. Step three: name the exact term. Step four: for two ways, use a different child or behaviour each time. For two marks per way, one mark is the behaviour and one is the linked term. The trap is mixing the theories, like saying Piaget kept thought and speech separate.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Frances, seven months, shuts her mouth, shakes her head and knocks the spoon away. Her sister Tamina, eighteen months, says cup and spoon as she walks away. Explain two ways Vygotsky can account for their actions. Four marks. Way one: Frances. She has no words, but she clearly means no. She is thinking without language. Way two: Tamina. She says cup and spoon, but she is not talking to anyone. She names what she sees. The words have no thought behind them. A tempting wrong route is to say thinking comes first, then language follows. That is Piaget. The question names Vygotsky, so it earns nothing. The exam answer. Each way: the child's behaviour, then the term explained. Each way earns one mark for the behaviour and one for the Vygotsky term. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is mixing up the theories. Students say Piaget saw thinking and speech as two separate systems. That is Vygotsky. Piaget said thinking comes first.

Your turn. Now you try. Peter is nine months old and cannot say the word drink. He points to his drink and squeals. Explain how he knows he needs a drink without any language, using Vygotsky. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Peter points and squeals to get his drink. He is thinking and solving a problem without words. Vygotsky calls this pre-linguistic thought. Thought and language begin as separate systems, so Peter can understand he needs a drink before he has any words. A common wrong answer calls his squealing pre-intellectual language, or says he has no schema. But Peter has a clear aim. That means thought is present. Pre-intellectual language has no thought behind it. So, name the theory. Quote the child's behaviour. Use the exact term, explained.
