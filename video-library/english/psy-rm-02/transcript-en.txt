Idea. Imagine a test of whether a sports drink makes runners faster. One runner wears trainers and one wears heavy boots. If the boot runner is slower, was it the drink? We cannot tell. Anything else that could change the result is an extraneous variable. There are two kinds. Participant variables are differences between the people, such as age, gender, intelligence or mood. Situational variables come from the setting or procedure, such as noise, temperature, time of day or instructions. Researchers control these. A standardised procedure means everyone gets the same instructions, materials, timing and setting. Single-blind means participants do not know the aim or which condition they are in. Double-blind means the researcher does not know either. The design matters too. Independent measures uses different people in each condition. Repeated measures uses the same people in all of them. Matched pairs uses pairs of similar people. These designs handle participant differences differently. This type of question asks you to describe or give a variable, a control or a design, and fit it to the study.

Method. Step one: name the kind of variable or control, and say what it means. Step two: pick an example that fits this study. Step three: say how it could change the result, or how the control stops that. The trap is mixing up the two kinds, or giving the IV as an extraneous variable. People are participant variables. Setting is situational. The IV is neither.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Thomas studies obedience. Sixty people are told to stay silent by a man in a uniform or a man in jeans. Thomas counts how many obey. We describe one participant variable he may have considered. It is worth two marks. Step one. Say what a participant variable is. It is a feature of the people in the study, apart from the IV, that could change the results. Step two. Choose one that fits obedience. Age is a good one. Older people may be more used to following orders from someone in uniform. Step three. Say how it could change the DV. Older people might obey more in either condition. So the number who obey would change because of age, not just the uniform. A tempting wrong route is to write the uniform or the jeans. That is the IV, because Thomas changed it on purpose. A participant variable is a different feature of the people. Now the exam-ready answer. Participant variables are personal characteristics of the people, apart from the IV, that might change the results. Thomas might have considered age, because older people might obey more whatever the condition. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is naming a participant variable with no link to the study. Always say how it could change the results.

Your turn. Now you try. Andrew showed participants twenty trigrams, which are groups of three letters, such as X Q B, and asked them to recall them. He used a standardised procedure. Give two ways he could have done this. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Standardised means the same for everyone. Way one: read the same instructions to every participant. Way two: show every trigram for the same length of time, such as two seconds each. Two different ways earn two marks. A common wrong answer says use participants of the same age. That controls the people, not the procedure, so it does not answer the question. Another is just saying he used a standardised procedure, which repeats the question. So name the kind of variable or control and define it. Give an example from the study. Say how it affects the result.
