Idea. A photo is flat, yet you can tell the tree is behind the boy. Your brain reads depth from clues. Depth cues are clues that let us see a flat image on the retina as a three-dimensional world. Monocular cues are depth cues that work with just one eye. There are five monocular cues to know. Superimposition. Relative size. Linear perspective. Texture gradient. And height in the plane. Superimposition means overlap. An object partly hidden behind another is judged to be further away. Relative size means that of two similar objects, the one that looks smaller is judged to be further away. Linear perspective is when parallel lines, like roads or railway tracks, seem to meet at a vanishing point on the horizon. Texture gradient is when close surfaces show clear, coarse detail, and distant surfaces look finer, smoother and blurred. The fifth cue is height in the plane. Objects nearer the horizon line look further away than objects lower in the scene. A question gives a scene, and asks you to name a cue and explain how it shows depth.

Method. Step one: name the cue and define it in one sentence. Step two: point to the exact objects in the scene, which looks nearer and which looks further. Step three: join them with the word so. One mark is for the cue, explained. One mark is for the objects in the scene. The traps are defining the cue without saying how it shows distance, and mixing up relative size with size constancy.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Hakim is learning to draw landscapes. His teacher shows him how to use linear perspective for buildings and roads. We must explain how it could help Hakim. Two marks. Step one: define the cue. Linear perspective is when parallel lines, like the two edges of a road, seem to get closer together as they go into the distance. They meet at a vanishing point on the horizon. The closer together the lines, the further away that part looks. Step two: apply it to Hakim. He can draw the two edges of each road as lines that slope towards one vanishing point. He can slope the tops and bottoms of the buildings to the same point. So the road seems to run away into the distance. A tempting wrong route is to say Hakim should draw the far buildings smaller. That is relative size, a different cue. Another is to say the lines just help him draw straight. Reject both. The question names linear perspective, and you must say how it shows depth. The exam answer. Define the cue. Then apply it to Hakim's drawing, and say it gives depth. One mark for explaining that lines meeting in the distance show depth. One mark for applying it to Hakim's roads and buildings. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is defining the cue without saying how it shows distance. Saying the picture has texture is not enough. You must say detail gets finer and smoother as distance grows.

Your turn. Now you try. Describe how superimposition creates depth perception. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Superimposition means one object overlaps another and partly hides it. The brain judges the partly hidden object to be further away, and the whole object in front to be nearer. For example, if a person stands in front of a car and hides part of it, the car looks further away. A common wrong answer says superimposition is when objects overlap, and stops there. That does not say how it shows depth. Some students also reverse it, and say the hidden object is nearer. So, name and define the cue. Point to the objects. Join them with so.
