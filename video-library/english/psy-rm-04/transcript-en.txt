Idea. Imagine a classmate shares your private test results with the whole school without asking. You would feel bad. Psychologists must avoid that. Ethics are the rules that protect the people who take part in research. Informed consent means people agree to take part after being told what the study involves. For children under sixteen, a parent must agree. Deception means misleading participants. It should be avoided, and if it is needed, the researcher must debrief. A debrief is an explanation at the end of the true aim, and a check that the person is all right. Confidentiality means keeping personal details private, for example using numbers or false names instead of real names. Right to withdraw means people can leave at any time and ask for their data to be removed. Protection means people leave in the same state they arrived, with no physical or psychological harm. This type of question names a study and asks you to give or describe ways the researcher could be ethical. Each way must be a real action in that study.

Method. Step one: name the ethical issue the question is about. Step two: say what the researcher would actually do in this study. Marks go to real actions, not just the name of the issue. Step three: if the question says give two ways, make them clearly different. Two versions of the same point earn only one mark. The trap is mixing up confidentiality and protection from harm.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Matthew interviews six patients with depression, before and after drug treatment. We give two ways he could keep the patients' details confidential. It is worth two marks, one for each different way. Step one. Confidentiality means keeping personal details private. So ask: how could the patients be identified, and how can we stop that? Names are one way. Way one. Matthew could use a number instead of each patient's name in his notes and results. Then nobody reading can tell who said what. Way two is different. Matthew could store the interview records securely, such as a locked cabinet or a password-protected file, so only he can read them. This is about who has access, not about names. A tempting wrong route is to write do not use names, and then use a false name. Those are the same idea, so you earn only one mark. Now the exam-ready answer. One: Matthew could give each patient a number rather than using their name in his results. Two: Matthew could store the interview records in a locked cabinet so that only he can see them. Common mistake. A common mistake is to name an issue without saying how to deal with it. Another is mixing up confidentiality with protection from harm. Confidentiality is about privacy.

Your turn. Now you try. James tests a brain-damaged patient on word recall, and will publish his investigation. Describe how he can maintain the patient's confidentiality. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Confidentiality means keeping the patient's identity private. In the published report, James can replace the patient's name with a number or false name. He should also leave out details that could identify the patient, such as address or workplace. A common wrong answer says James should ask the patient for consent. That is informed consent, a different issue. It does not keep identity private, so it loses the mark. So name the issue. Say what the researcher does in this study. For two ways, make them different.
