Idea. You see a blurry shape far away on the football pitch. You think, that looks like my friend, because of the way it walks. You used what you already know to make a guess. This is Gregory's constructivist theory. Gregory said perception is top-down. Perception means how we make sense of what we see. Top-down means the brain builds what we see from the sensory input, which is what reaches the eyes, plus prior knowledge, which is what we already know. The brain's best guess is called a perceptual hypothesis. It is then tested against the input. Past experience decides which hypothesis is chosen. If two guesses fit equally well, the brain cannot settle. So an ambiguous figure flips, and we see only one reading at a time. The theory has one big strength. It explains illusions, and why expectation and culture change what we see. But illusions are flat artificial drawings. Gibson argued real scenes are rich in information. And it cannot explain how babies with little experience see depth. The question gives you a person who sees something. You explain it using prior knowledge and hypotheses, or you assess the theory.

Method. Step one: pick the Gregory idea, such as prior knowledge or a perceptual hypothesis. Step two: say what the person saw and which past experience they used. Step three: justify with Gregory, nineteen seventy. The trap is using Gibson's optic flow, which is the wrong theory. Nine mark questions assess the theory. Aim for three points each of knowledge, application and evaluation, then finish with a judgement.

Worked example. Here is a real two mark question. Jacob says Carly saw a car moving slowly because she already understands speed and distance. Carly says she used optic flow. We must explain one reason that supports Jacob, using a theory. Jacob talks about prior understanding. That is prior knowledge, so the theory is Gregory's, not Carly's optic flow. Optic flow is Gibson's idea. Now the point. Carly has seen many cars before. Her brain uses that past experience to guess how fast a car at that distance is going. That is her perceptual hypothesis. A tempting wrong route is to explain it with optic flow. That supports Carly, not Jacob. Here is the answer. Carly has prior knowledge of how fast cars move at different distances. Gregory, nineteen seventy, says perception is top-down, so her brain combines this knowledge with what she sees to judge the speed.
