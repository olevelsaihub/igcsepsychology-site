Idea. You score six, eight and ten in three tests. Share the marks out equally. Add them up to get twenty four, then divide by three. You get eight. That is the mean. The mean is the total of all the values divided by how many values there are. Count carefully. A repeated value counts every time it appears. If the scores are four, four and seven, there are three values, not two. The total is fifteen, and fifteen divided by three is five. Sometimes the question says give your answer to two decimal places. Decimal places are the digits after the decimal point. Look at the third digit. If it is five or more, round up. Two point three four six rounds to two point three five. The mean uses every score, so one extreme score can pull it up. This question type says calculate the mean, or state how you would calculate it.

Method. Step one: add up every value in the set the question names. Step two: count how many values there are. Step three: divide the total by that count, and round as instructed. The trap is the wrong divisor. Divide by the number of values, not by the number of different values.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Michelle compares the long-term memory of fifteen year olds and fifty five year olds. We calculate the mean number of pictures recalled by the fifty five year olds, to two decimal places. It is worth two marks. Step one. Pick the right column. Only the fifty five year olds count. Here are their seven scores. Step two. Add them. Sixteen plus twenty eight plus twenty seven plus thirty four plus thirty five plus twenty six plus twenty one. The total is one hundred and eighty seven. Step three. Count the scores. There are seven participants, A to G. So we divide one hundred and eighty seven by seven. Step four. Round to two decimal places. The third decimal place is four, so we round down. The mean is twenty six point seven one. A tempting wrong route is to add both columns and divide by fourteen. That gives twenty eight. But that is the mean of everyone, not the fifty five year olds. Now the exam-ready answer. The total of the fifty five year olds' scores is one hundred and eighty seven. One hundred and eighty seven divided by seven is twenty six point seven one. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is rounding wrongly. Look at the third decimal place. If a number is two point three four six, the six tells you to round up. Cutting the number off at two decimal places gives two point three four, which is wrong.

Your turn. Now you try. Ryan asked students in four schools about their mindset. A fixed mindset means believing your ability cannot change. Calculate the mean number of students with a fixed mindset. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Use the fixed mindset column only: four hundred and fifty eight, two hundred and eleven, six hundred and forty one, and one hundred and eighty nine. Add them to get one thousand, four hundred and ninety nine. There are four schools, so divide by four. One thousand, four hundred and ninety nine divided by four is three hundred and seventy four point seven five. A common wrong answer uses the growth mindset column. That total is two thousand, four hundred and seventy nine, and the mean is six hundred and nineteen point seven five. It is the wrong column, so it loses the mark. So add every value. Count the values. Divide, and round as asked.
