Idea. Think about your friends. One is always funny. One is always shy. These lasting ways of behaving are called traits. A trait is a steady feature of someone's personality. Personality is the whole pattern of traits that makes you who you are. In nineteen thirty-six, Gordon Allport said traits come in three levels. A cardinal trait rules a person's whole life, and it is rare. Central traits are the main ones that describe someone, about five to ten. Secondary traits only show up in some situations, like getting nervous before a test. In nineteen forty-six, Raymond Cattell used factor analysis, a way of sorting many words into a few groups, to cut a huge list of trait words down to sixteen personality factors. Each factor is a scale with two opposite ends, like reserved and warm. The sixteen P F is a questionnaire. It is self-report, which means people answer questions about themselves. It is standardised, so everyone gets the same questions. But people may choose answers that look good. That is called social desirability. A question may ask you to describe a way of measuring personality, apply a trait level, or judge the sixteen P F. You name the idea, say how it works, and link it to the person.

Method. Step one: name the measure or trait level that fits the clue. Words like central, secondary or scale tell you which one. This earns the knowledge mark. Step two: say how it works. Step three: link it to the person, using details from the question. That is the second mark. The trap is mixing up cardinal and central. A cardinal trait is one rare trait that dominates a whole life. Central traits are several main traits.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Patrick's boss says he is tense and impersonal. Patrick disagrees, so he takes Cattell's sixteen P F. It says he is relaxed, tough and caring. We must explain one reason the test may have measured him wrongly. Two marks. Step one: the reason. Patrick wanted to prove his boss wrong. So he may have answered in a socially desirable way, picking answers that made him look good. Step two: justify it with Cattell. The sixteen P F is self-report. His answers put him on scales, such as reserved to warm. If the answers are not honest, the scores are wrong. A tempting wrong route is to say the test is unreliable because it is a questionnaire. Reject it. It gives no reason and does not use Patrick. Now write it out. Reason first, then the Cattell justification. One mark for the reason. One mark for the justification using the sixteen P F. Common mistake. A common mistake is to say the sixteen P F measures sixteen types of person. It does not. It measures sixteen factors, and each one is a scale with two ends.

Your turn. Now you try. Jim is confident and well organised, but before a ten-minute presentation he feels nervous. Explain two ways traits can account for the change, using Allport. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Way one: nervousness is a secondary trait. Allport said these only show in some situations, and talking to the class is one. Way two: confident and organised are central traits, Jim's main ones. They show in most situations, so the nerves are the exception. The common wrong answer says Jim's personality has changed. It has not. Only the situation changed. That loses the marks. So name the idea, say how it works, and link it to the person. Use that order in every personality answer.
