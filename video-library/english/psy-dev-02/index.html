Read the narration

Idea. Stand on one side of a table. A friend stands on the other. Do you both see the same thing? Young children often think so. They only see things from their own point of view. That is called egocentrism. In nineteen fifty-six, Piaget and Inhelder tested this with the Three mountains task. They used a model of three mountains. A doll was moved to different places around it. Each child looked at ten pictures and chose the one showing what the doll could see. Children aged about four to six usually chose their own view. That shows egocentrism. By about seven to nine, most chose the doll's view. They could decentre, which means take another person's view. This supports Piaget. Egocentrism belongs to the pre-operational stage, which runs from about two to seven years old. Questions come in three kinds. A one-mark multiple choice on the numbers. A child behaving in an egocentric way. Or a table where you draw a conclusion. Remember three mountains, ten pictures, one hundred children.

Method. For multiple choice, recall the exact numbers. Three mountains. Ten pictures. One hundred children, thirty-three of them aged eight to nine. The trap is mixing ten pictures with three mountains. For a scenario, state the finding, which is that young children chose their own view. Then link it to the child and name the stage, pre-operational. For a table, state what it shows about perspective taking by age. Then quote numbers. A conclusion needs the evidence.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Pupils aged six, eight and ten wear a headset and see three buildings. They say what a person on the opposite side of town would see. Table 1 shows their answers. We explain one conclusion about cognitive development. Two marks. Here is Table 1. It gives the number of pupils who said each building would be at the front. Of the six-year-olds, twenty-six said the triangular building and four said the rectangular one. At eight, it was thirteen and seventeen. At ten, it was three and twenty-seven. Step one: read the data. The triangular building at the front is the pupils' own view. Twenty-six of thirty six-year-olds gave it. Only three of thirty ten-year-olds did. Step two: draw the conclusion. Perspective taking improves with age, and most children can see another person's view by ten. Step three: support it with figures. Twenty-seven of thirty ten-year-olds gave the other person's view, but only four of thirty six-year-olds. A tempting wrong route is to just describe the table. Reject it. Saying twenty-six six-year-olds chose the triangle is not a conclusion about development. Now write it out. The conclusion, then the figures. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to mix up the numbers. The model has three mountains. The children chose from ten pictures. Do not swap them.

Your turn. Now you try. Two one-mark questions on the study's numbers. Part a asks how many mountains were used. Part b asks how many children aged eight to nine were in the sample. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Part a. The model had three mountains, so the answer is C. Part b. Of the one hundred children, thirty-three were aged eight to nine, so the answer is D. The common wrong answer to part b is C, thirty. It looks close, and Leanne had thirty pupils at each age. But the study had thirty-three children aged eight to nine. Close is not enough for the mark. So for multiple choice, recall the exact number. For a scenario, state the finding, link it to the child and name the stage. For a table, give the conclusion, then the figures.
