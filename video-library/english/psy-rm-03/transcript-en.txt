Idea. A school wants to know what students think about lunches. It cannot ask all five hundred students. The whole group it wants to learn about is the target population. The few people it actually asks are the sample. Random sampling means every member of the target population has an equal chance of being picked, for example names drawn from a hat. Stratified sampling splits the population into subgroups, then picks from each in proportion to its size. Volunteer sampling means people choose to take part, usually by replying to an advert. Opportunity sampling means using whoever is available and willing at the time, such as the first people you meet. Random and stratified samples are usually more representative. That means they match the whole population. Volunteer and opportunity samples are quicker, but more likely to be biased. This question type names a sampling method and asks how a researcher could use it.

Method. Step one: say in one clause what the method is. Step two: describe the practical steps this researcher would take, using the people and place in the question. Step three: check the steps match the method. The trap is confusing random with opportunity. Picking people at random in the street is not random sampling. It is opportunity, because you only reach people who happen to be there. Random needs a list of everyone and a chance method.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Jenna tests whether stress affects memory. She recruits ten people from her university. We describe how she could use opportunity sampling to gather them. It is worth two marks. Step one. Define it. Opportunity sampling means using people who are available and willing at the time. Step two. Apply it to Jenna. Her people are university students. Jenna could go to the university library one afternoon. She could ask the first ten students she sees to take part. A tempting wrong route is to say Jenna picks ten students from the university register using a number generator. That gives everyone an equal chance. It is random sampling, not opportunity. Now the exam-ready answer. Opportunity sampling means using people who are available and willing at the time. Jenna could go to her university library one afternoon and ask the first ten students she sees to take part in her memory study. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is giving the definition only. The question says how Jenna could use it. You must say what Jenna would do.

Your turn. Now you try. Jason is planning a questionnaire about social media use by people of different ages. Describe how he could use stratified sampling. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Stratified sampling splits the population into subgroups, and picks from each in proportion to its size. For Jason, the subgroups are age groups, such as under twenty, twenty to forty, and over forty. If the over forties are half of the population, half the sample comes from them. A common wrong answer says to ask a few people from each age group, with no proportions. That is not stratified. The sample sizes must match the size of each group in the population, so that answer loses the mark. So define the method. Use the researcher's people and place. Check it matches the method.
