Idea. A friend says her new revision app is brilliant, because she tried it for one evening. Would you believe her? You would ask who tried it, and how it was tested. Judging a study works the same way. A study is a planned test that a psychologist runs to answer a question. Three words help us judge. Reliability means you would get the same results if you repeated the study. Validity means the study really measures what it says it measures. Generalisability means the findings could apply to other people, not just those in the study. A strength makes the results more trustworthy. A baseline is a measure taken before anything changes, so you can see the change. A control group is a group that gets no treatment, to compare with. A real-life setting also helps. A weakness makes the results less trustworthy. A small sample, meaning only a few people, is one. An extraneous variable is another. That is something you did not plan to change, which could still affect the results. The question asks for a strength and a weakness of one study.

Method. Step one: find a detail of this study, such as who, where, what task or how it was measured. Step two: say if it is a strength or a weakness. Step three: explain the effect on reliability, validity or generalisability, linked to the topic. The trap is a general point, like the sample was small, with no detail from the study. It gains little.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Beth is testing her eight-month-old daughter at home. We explain one strength and one weakness of her investigation. Four marks. Strength. The detail is that Beth tested the baby at home, in her own living room. A baby in a familiar place is relaxed, so she acts naturally. The result then shows what she can really do, which makes it more valid. Weakness. The detail is that Beth tested only one baby, her own daughter. Other eight-month-olds may behave differently. So we cannot say the result applies to all babies. That is poor generalisability. A tempting wrong route is to write, the sample was small, and stop. That could be about any study. Name the one baby, her own daughter. The exam answer. One mark for each point and one for each reason. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is a reason that only says more valid or less reliable. That is a label, not a reason. Say what actually happens in the study.

Your turn. Now you try. Ishfaq studies one patient with damage to the corpus callosum, the band of nerve fibres joining the two halves of the brain. Explain one strength and one weakness. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Strength. Ishfaq used both interviews and laboratory tests. Open questions give detailed answers in the patient's own words, and the tests give scores. Two sources give a fuller picture, so the findings are more valid. Weakness. He studied only one patient, so the findings may not apply to other people with the same damage. A common wrong answer is, only one patient, so it is not reliable. That mixes up two words. One patient is a problem for generalising to other people. Reliability is about getting the same result when you repeat the study. So find a detail, label it, and explain the effect on the result.
