Idea. Suppose you want to know if a new pair of trainers helps you jump higher. You could test it in a sports lab, in the school playground, or wait until a school changes its shoes by itself. Those are the three kinds of experiment. An experiment tests cause and effect by changing one thing, the IV, and measuring another, the DV. In a natural experiment the researcher does not change the IV. It changes naturally. For example, Becker and colleagues studied eating behaviours in Fiji as television arrived, and three years later. They did not bring the television. They only measured the eating behaviour. A field experiment example is Godden and Baddeley, nineteen seventy-five. Scuba divers learned word lists underwater or on land, then recalled them in the same or a different place. They recalled more words when the places matched. The setting was real, and the researchers changed the IV. Each method has strengths and weaknesses. A lab gives high control, and is easy to repeat. But it is artificial. That means low ecological validity, which is how true to real life the findings are. Participants may also guess the aim and change their behaviour. These are demand characteristics. Natural experiments let us study things that would be unethical or impossible to change. But the researcher has no control over who is in each group, or over extraneous variables. These are other things that could change the result. This question type asks you to identify, state or evaluate an experiment method.

Method. Step one: find the setting and who changes the IV. These two clues separate the three methods. Step two: for short questions, give one accurate feature or choose the matching option. For a twelve-mark question, write four paragraphs. First describe the method. Then link it to the named study. Then weigh strengths against weaknesses. Last, give a justified judgement. A long description with no judgement cannot reach the top level.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. James studies the effect of brain damage on memory. He asks a patient to come to the university, to a room he has set up, for a test of word recall. We must identify the method from four options. It is worth one mark. Step one. Find the setting. James set up a room at the university. That is an artificial, controlled setting, not the patient's everyday life. Step two. Find what James does. He gives a test of word recall. He does not send a list of written questions, or ask spoken ones. So A, questionnaire, and D, interview, do not fit. A tempting wrong route is C, natural experiment, because James did not cause the brain damage. But the clue here is the setting. He tests in a controlled room he set up. That is a laboratory. So the answer is B, laboratory experiment. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is calling a study a natural experiment when the researcher changed the IV. If the researcher changes the IV, it is a laboratory or a field experiment.

Your turn. Becca will use a laboratory experiment to look for differences in brain functioning between males and females. State one feature of a laboratory experiment. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Any one accurate feature earns the mark. It takes place in a controlled, artificial setting. Or the researcher changes the IV and measures the DV. Or it is easy to repeat, because the procedure is standardised. A common wrong answer says it takes place in a natural, real-life setting. That is a field experiment. A laboratory is artificial. So find the setting. Find who changes the IV. Name the method, and give one accurate feature.
