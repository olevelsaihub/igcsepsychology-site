Idea. A friend sends a message with a smiley face. Does the smile show they are happy, or do the eyes? Psychologists found that culture changes which part of the face we trust. In two thousand and seven, Yuki and colleagues compared how students in Japan and America judge emotion. There were ninety-five Japanese students and one hundred and eighteen American students. Culture means the shared habits and beliefs of a group of people. The emoticons, and edited photos of faces, had happy, neutral or sad eyes and mouths in different mixes. Students rated how happy each one looked. The result. Japanese participants gave more weight to the eyes. American participants gave more weight to the mouth. The explanation is that in Japan people are encouraged to hide emotions. The mouth is easy to control, but the eyes are harder, so the eyes are trusted more. A weakness is that students and emoticons may not match everyday judgements. A question names two people from different cultures and asks why they read emotion differently.

Method. Step one: work out which person is Eastern and which is Western. Step two: say which cue each would use, eyes or mouth. Step three: say what each concludes. Step four: justify with the Yuki finding. One mark is the reason, which is different cues. One mark is the study justification. The trap is reversing the finding. Japanese rely on eyes. Americans rely on the mouth.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Callum notices Emma did not smile much, and wonders if she is upset. Their friend Itsuki, who has just returned from Japan, says she seemed happy. We must explain one reason they disagreed, using Yuki. Two marks. Step one: sort the people by culture. Callum is from England, so Western. Itsuki has come from Japan, so Eastern. Step two: what each concluded. Emma did not smile, so Callum, reading her mouth, thought she was upset. Itsuki, reading her eyes, thought she looked happy. A tempting wrong route is to reverse the finding, saying Americans rely on the eyes. Reject it. In Yuki, Japanese relied on the eyes, and Americans on the mouth. The exam answer. The reason, then the Yuki finding. One mark for the reason, different cues. One mark for the Yuki justification. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is naming the study without its result. Saying Yuki studied emotion explains nothing. You must say what Yuki found: the Japanese used the eyes, the Americans the mouth.

Answer. Kenji lives in Japan, so he mainly judges emotion by the eyes. The smiley shows happiness through the mouth, which is what Americans focus on. Yuki found Japanese rely more on the eyes, so Kenji is unsure what it means. A common wrong answer says Kenji is unsure because people in Japan do not use emoticons. That is not in the study.
