Idea. Imagine a school votes on a new canteen menu, but only asks one class. Would the menu suit the whole school? Maybe not. The whole group a study is about is the target population. The people actually studied are the sample. A sample is representative if it reflects the target population. Generalisability means how far the findings apply to people beyond the sample. A study of one person, one gender, one place or one culture is hard to generalise. Its findings might be true only for that narrow group. An improvement here changes who or where is studied. You could use a larger sample, include both males and females, use several places, or study other cultures. Each change has a reason. This question type says: explain one improvement. The study usually has a narrow sample. You name a change to who or where, then explain how it helps.

Method. Step one: spot what is narrow about the sample or setting. Step two: suggest one specific change for this study. Step three: explain how the change makes the findings more representative or easier to generalise, in the study's own topic. The trap is a vague improvement, such as being more careful. Name an actual change to who or where is studied. Then link the reason to this study.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Sandy tests the brain function of Region G. She tests one female patient with damage to this region. The patient does well on recall and pressure, but badly on colour naming. We explain one improvement Sandy could have made. It is worth two marks. Step one. Spot what is narrow. Sandy tested only one patient, and she was female. Step two. Suggest the change. Sandy could test several patients with damage to Region G, both males and females. Step three. Justify it. With many patients, she could see whether poor colour naming happens to everyone with this damage. So she could be more confident that Region G causes it, and that it is not just true of her one patient. A tempting wrong route is to say: use more participants. It names a change, but gives no reason. The second mark is for the justification, so that answer would earn one mark at most. Now the exam-ready answer. Sandy could have tested several patients with damage to Region G, including males and females. Then she could be more confident that poor colour naming is caused by damage to Region G in people generally, and is not just something unique to her one female patient. Common mistake. A common mistake is a reason that does not link to the change. Because it is better is not a reason. Say what the change lets the researcher show, in this study.

Your turn. Now you try. Spatial ability is skill in judging space and direction. Josh tests it using a map task. Explain one improvement Josh could have made to his investigation. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. First spot what is narrow. Josh tested just one female and one male. A difference between two people may be personal, not about sex. Now the change and the reason. Josh could test many females and many males, for example thirty of each. Then a difference would be more likely to show a real sex difference, and the findings would apply to males and females generally. A common wrong answer says use a bigger sample, because it is better. It names a change but gives no reason linked to sex differences. That earns one mark at most. So spot the narrow sample.
