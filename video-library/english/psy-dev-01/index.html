Piaget's stages of cognitive development

Idea. A baby does not look for a toy that is hidden. A teenager can plan their future. Jean Piaget said thinking grows through four stages, always in the same order. This is called cognitive development, which means how thinking develops. In the sensorimotor stage, babies learn through senses and movement. They gain object permanence, which means knowing an object still exists when hidden. In the pre-operational stage, children are egocentric and cannot yet conserve. Conservation means knowing that an amount stays the same when only its look changes. In the concrete operational stage, children think logically about real objects and can conserve. In the formal operational stage, from about eleven, people think in an abstract way and about hypothetical situations, which means things that have not happened. Piaget also described schemas. A schema is a mental framework of knowledge. Assimilation is fitting new information into an existing schema. Accommodation is changing the schema. Equilibrium is the balance reached afterwards. A question describes what a child does. You name the stage or process, and link it to the child. You may also judge the theory: it is supported by the Three mountains task, but later research says Piaget underestimated young children.

Method. Step one: spot the clue in the behaviour. Step two: write one sentence naming the stage or process and its key feature. Step three: write a second sentence linking it to what the child did. Each clue points to one idea. Searching for a hidden toy means object permanence. Saying a longer row has more means pre-operational. Thinking about the future means formal operational. An old word for a new thing means assimilation. The trap is defining the stage but never linking it to the child. That earns one mark out of two. Always use the child's details.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Children are shown a picture of a fourteen-year-old who lives with parents, eats healthily and works hard. They are asked what the person might be like in twenty years. We must explain what someone in the formal operational stage would say. Two marks. Step one: the clue. They are asked about twenty years ahead. That has not happened yet, so it needs hypothetical thinking. Step two: state the feature. From about eleven, people reason in an abstract way about situations that have not happened. Step three: apply it. Use the facts given. This person works hard and eats healthily, so they could predict good health, good qualifications and a well-paid job. A tempting wrong route is to say the child would answer that nobody can know. Reject it. That is how younger children think. A formal operational thinker reasons about the future. Now write it out. The stage, then the prediction. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is putting conservation in the pre-operational stage. Children cannot conserve yet at that stage. They reach conservation in the concrete operational stage.

Your turn. Now you try. Three-year-old Shannon sees a bird, and her mum says bird. Next day she sees an aeroplane and says bird. Explain why she made the mistake, using Piaget. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Shannon has a bird schema, which is something flying in the sky. She used assimilation. She fitted the aeroplane into her bird schema. She has not yet accommodated, so she has not changed the schema to include aeroplanes. The common wrong answer says she is only three, so she is silly. That names no idea from Piaget and loses the mark. So spot the clue, name the stage or process, and link it to the child.
