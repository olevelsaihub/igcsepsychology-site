Idea. Think about learning football. You do not get good just by watching. You practise again and again. Daniel Willingham is a psychologist who used memory research to explain how children learn. His learning theory gives advice about what teachers and parents should do. His first idea is that factual knowledge comes before skill. A skill is something you can do well, like solving problems. You need background facts before you can think critically about a topic. His second idea is that practice and effort are essential. Repeated practice makes a skill automatic. That frees up working memory, which is the small space where you hold things while you think. Teachers use these ideas for three kinds of development. Cognitive means thinking and learning. Use practice, with tasks that are hard but achievable. Physical means movement. Repeat the movement with feedback. Social means behaviour with others. Show good behaviour and let children practise it. Now evaluate. A strength is research support: expert violinists had done far more deliberate practice. A weakness is that the advice is general and may not suit every learner. A question gives a class or child and asks what a teacher could do.

Method. Step one: decide which development the question is about: cognitive, physical or social. Step two: state the Willingham idea that fits, such as practice with effort or modelling good behaviour. Step three: turn the idea into a specific activity, using the task named in the question. A vague tip like study more earns nothing. The trap is also writing skill before knowledge. That is the reverse of Willingham. For a data question, say what the trend shows about practice or difficulty, and quote the figures. One mark is the conclusion. One is the evidence.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Mr Harris tests his class on the order of the planets at the end of each of four days. Table 1 shows the results. We explain how he could help them improve, using Willingham. Two marks. Here is Table 1. The average score was thirty-two per cent of planets in the correct order on days one and two, and thirty-three per cent on days three and four. The mean time stayed about twenty-one seconds. So the class is hardly improving. Step one: state the idea. Willingham says a new skill improves with repeated, effortful practice. Step two: a specific activity. Mr Harris could set a short daily task where pupils put planet cards in order from the Sun, check their answers, and repeat until quick and correct. A tempting wrong route is to tell the class to study more. Reject it. It is vague and names no Willingham idea. Say what the practice is, and why it works. Now write it out. The idea, then the activity. One mark for the idea. One mark for the activity. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to reverse Willingham. He said factual knowledge comes before skill, not the other way round.

Your turn. Now you try. Molly and Mack snatch toys, push other children and shout in their faces. Their teacher wants to improve their social development. Explain one strategy, using Willingham. Write your answer, then choose Continue to Answer.

Answer. Willingham says social development grows when children see good social behaviour and then practise it. So Mrs Kumar could show them how to ask for a toy politely and take turns. Then she could get them to practise this in play. The common wrong answer says tell them off. That names no Willingham idea, so it loses the mark. So match the type of development, state the idea, and make it a specific activity.
