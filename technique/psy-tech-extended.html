Psychology / Extended answers (9 and 12 marks): assess, evaluate, discuss

Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Psychology · 1PS0

Extended answers (9 and 12 marks): assess, evaluate, discuss

Assess, evaluate or discuss how well a theory, study, treatment or method explains a scenario, often with a named theory or 'two areas of psychology'.

Command words: Assess, Evaluate, Discuss, JustifyUsually 9 or 12 marksAbout 12-13 minutes for 9 marks; 16-18 minutes for 12 marks

What Pearson asks

Assess, evaluate or discuss how well a theory, study, treatment or method explains a scenario, often with a named theory or 'two areas of psychology'.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked: marks are split equally between knowledge and understanding (AO1), application to the scenario (AO2) and evaluation with a judgement (AO3). Answers showing only knowledge are capped at Level 1; missing a required element (e.g. two areas) caps at Level 2.

Method

  1. Plan for 1-2 minutes: list 3 knowledge points, 3 scenario links and 3 evaluation points.

    Why: Each assessment objective carries a third of the marks.

  2. Explain the theory or concept accurately with key terms.

    Why: AO1 knowledge.

  3. Weave in specific scenario details throughout, not just once.

    Why: Level 3 needs sustained application.

  4. Evaluate with supporting and contradicting evidence or alternative explanations.

    Why: AO3 needs competing arguments.

  5. Finish with a reasoned judgement that answers 'how well'.

    Why: Level 3 requires a judgement.

Answer frame

Intro: what the theory says. Paragraphs: theory point -> scenario detail -> evaluation (evidence for/against or alternative). Conclusion: overall, it explains ... well/partly because ..., but ...

Common errors

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Describing the theory only, with no scenario.
  • Mentioning the scenario once at the end.
  • One-sided evaluation with no counter-argument.
  • No final judgement.
  • Ignoring a requirement such as 'two areas of psychology'.

Worked illustration

June 2024 · Paper 2 · Q27(whole)

The complete original question is shown; the illustration answers part (whole).

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Gregory's (1970) constructivist theory says perception is top-down: we receive incomplete sensory information and use past experience and expectations to build a hypothesis, our best guess, about what we are seeing. Because the guess depends on what we already know and feel, emotions and expectations can bias it, so two people can perceive the same scene differently.

This fits Shamila well. Her bag was stolen by someone in a dark hooded coat, so her past experience now makes her expect danger on the walk home. When she sees people with their hoods up, her hypothesis is that they are thieves, and she interprets glances as people staring at her bag. Her fear adds to this bias. Her friend has no such experience, so her friend's hypothesis is that the people are ordinary passers-by, which explains why the friend noticed nothing.

Support comes from visual illusions, for example the Necker cube, where the brain keeps switching between two guesses, showing that perception is an active hypothesis that can be wrong, just as Shamila's may be. The theory also explains individual differences, which matches the friends seeing the walk so differently.

However, Gibson's bottom-up theory argues the environment contains enough information for accurate perception without guessing. From this view Shamila may have correctly seen people looking at her bag, and the hooded person could have been a real threat. Illusion evidence also uses artificial drawings, so it may not show how perception works on a real street.

Overall, Gregory's theory explains Shamila's perception well, because her past theft and fear clearly shape what she thinks she sees and explain why her friend saw something different, although we cannot rule out that some of what she noticed was accurate.

Why it earns the marks

It gives accurate knowledge of the constructivist theory (AO1), uses Shamila's experience, fear and the friend's contrast throughout (AO2), and evaluates with illusion evidence, the bottom-up alternative and a limitation before a clear judgement (AO3), meeting Level 3.
