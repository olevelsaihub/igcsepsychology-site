  1. Pick two

    Match two behaviours to two different topics.

  2. Explain

    Describe each theory accurately.

  3. Apply

    Link each to details, using the person's name.

  4. Evaluate

    A named study, its finding, and a limitation.

  5. Judge

    How well do the two areas explain the behaviour?

Using two areas of psychology to explain a scenario (9 marks) · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Question 23 · part (whole) · 9 marks

*23 Giovanni was at school at lunchtime when his friend asked him if he was going to the concert of their favourite band the following summer. Giovanni said he had no money to pay for a ticket, so his friend suggested he get a part-time job. Giovanni’s friend said most of their friends had part-time jobs that fitted around school and were all saving their money to pay for a ticket to the concert. Giovanni went to his local shop and was successful in getting a job as a newspaper delivery person. He had to deliver newspapers to the same houses each day and remember a specific route which started and ended at the shop. When Giovanni was delivering newspapers one morning, he looked through the window of a house and saw an elderly person on the floor in distress. Giovanni tried to get into the house to help the person, but could not, so rang the emergency services who arrived soon after. The paramedics said the person was lucky that Giovanni had raised the alarm as they did not have any family so were unlikely to have been helped. Assess Giovanni’s behaviour using two areas of psychology that you have studied. (9)

Worked explanation

  1. Match behaviours: getting a job because most friends have one = conformity; learning the delivery route = memory.
  2. Explain conformity and the multi-store model (AO1).
  3. Apply each to Giovanni (AO2).
  4. Evaluate with Asch and Peterson and Peterson, including a limitation of each (AO3).
  5. Conclude.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Conformity to majority influence happens when a person changes what they do so that it matches what most of their group is doing, either to fit in (normative influence) or because they think the group knows best. Giovanni had not planned to get a job, but once his friend told him most of their friends had part-time jobs and were saving for the concert, he went and got one. He was following the majority so he would not be left out of the trip.

This is supported by Asch (1951), where participants gave obviously wrong answers about line lengths to match a group of confederates; around a third of answers on critical trials conformed and roughly three-quarters went along with the group on at least one trial. However, Asch used a trivial lab task with American male students, so it may not fully explain an important real-life decision like taking a job, where Giovanni also had his own reason (wanting a ticket). His behaviour may be partly conformity and partly personal choice.

Atkinson and Shiffrin's multi-store model (1968) says information enters the sensory register, attention moves it into short-term memory, and rehearsal transfers it into long-term memory, where it can last a lifetime. Giovanni had to learn which houses to deliver to and the exact route from the shop and back. Paying attention on his first rounds and repeating the same route every day would rehearse it until it was stored in long-term memory, so he can now do it without thinking.

The Petersons' 1959 trigram study backs up the importance of rehearsal: when participants were stopped from rehearsing trigrams by counting backwards, recall fell to about 10% after 18 seconds. This suggests Giovanni's daily repetition is what keeps the route in memory. On the other hand, the model is criticised as too simple: the K.F. case suggests short-term memory may have several separate stores, and remembering a route may rely on visual and spatial memory that the model does not separate out.

Overall, conformity explains why Giovanni took the job well, and the multi-store model explains how he learned his route, but both are supported by artificial lab research, so each gives only part of the explanation of his real-life behaviour.

How the marks are earned

Level 3: accurate AO1 on conformity and the multi-store model; sustained AO2 links to Giovanni's friends and route; AO3 uses Asch and Peterson and Peterson with limitations, leading to a balanced judgement. Two areas are used so no cap applies.
