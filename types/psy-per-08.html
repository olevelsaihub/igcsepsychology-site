Worked explanation

  1. AO1: procedure (12 figures, two name lists, control), results (around three quarters vs under half), conclusion.
  2. AO2: match Jesse's labelled group to the named groups and his no-label group to the control; ice cream cone example.
  3. AO3: similarities (control group, lab task), differences (fewer images, all-male, on-screen), memory vs perception.
  4. Judgement.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Carmichael, Hogan and Walter (1932) showed participants 12 ambiguous line drawings. As each appeared, one group heard a name from list 1 and another group a different name from list 2, for example the same figure called 'eyeglasses' or 'dumbbells', while a control group heard no names. When participants later redrew the figures, around three quarters of the drawings by the labelled groups looked like the object they had been told about, whereas under half of the control group's drawings resembled either name. They concluded that words heard at the time of seeing change how a shape is perceived and reproduced.

Jesse's study follows the same logic. His Group 1, like the labelled groups, heard descriptions such as 'ice cream cone' before seeing the triangle, and their drawings came out looking like cones rather than plain triangles. His Group 2 heard nothing, like the control group, and their drawings stayed close to the original shapes. So the original findings predict exactly the pattern Jesse got: the label pulls the reproduction towards the named object.

This gives good support, because both studies used controlled conditions, ambiguous figures and a comparison group without labels, so the difference between the groups can be put down to the words. However, Jesse's study differs: he used only six images shown on a screen and only 20 participants, all male, whereas the original used more figures and both sexes, so his results may be less reliable and less representative and the original may not explain them perfectly. Also, both tasks are artificial, and the label may have affected memory of the shape rather than perception itself, which questions what either study really shows.

Overall, Carmichael et al. account well for Jesse's results because his method and findings closely mirror theirs, but the smaller, all-male sample and fewer images mean the match is not complete.

How the marks are earned

Level 3: accurate procedure, findings and conclusion (AO1); sustained comparison of Jesse's two groups with the labelled and control groups (AO2); balanced evaluation of similarities and differences, then a final verdict (AO3).
