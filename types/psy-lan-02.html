The method

  1. Which version?

    Influences means relativism; decides means determinism.

  2. Define it

    One clear sentence on how language affects thought.

  3. Use the scenario

    Quote the exact words that cause the mix-up.

  4. Add evidence

    Boroditsky for time, Everett for number.

  5. Long answers

    Weigh the other version and give a judgement.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Swapping versions

    Relativism is the weak one; determinism the strong one.

  • ✗
    Wrong directions

    Mandarin vertical, English horizontal.

  • ✗
    Reversed theory

    Language shapes thought, not the other way round.

*40 Tyler is a lecturer at Dunarvon University. He is hoping to arrange a meeting with Zhang, a newly appointed lecturer who has recently arrived in the country. Tyler emails Zhang suggesting a meeting at three in the afternoon. He tells Zhang that he is happy to move the meeting forwards or backwards if this time is not convenient. Zhang replies that he would like the meeting to be moved above three in the afternoon if possible. Tyler is confused about what time Zhang wants the meeting to take place. Assess how well linguistic relativism can account for Tyler’s lack of understanding about what time to arrange the meeting for. (9)

Worked explanation

  1. AO1: define relativism and contrast it with determinism.
  2. AO2: Tyler uses forwards/backwards; Zhang uses above; this mismatch causes the confusion.
  3. AO2: Zhang still partly understood Tyler, showing influence rather than control.
  4. AO3: Boroditsky's horizontal/vertical evidence supports relativism.
  5. AO3: consider determinism and the fact that the confusion is easy to fix; judge.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Linguistic relativism is the weak version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. It claims the language we grow up with shapes how we think about and describe the world, so speakers of different languages may understand the same idea in different ways. Unlike linguistic determinism, it does not say language completely fixes our thinking; it only influences it.

This can explain Tyler's confusion. Tyler, an English speaker, thinks about time as a line running forwards and backwards, so he offered to move the meeting forwards or backwards. Zhang, who has recently arrived and may have Mandarin as his first language, asked for the meeting to go 'above' three o'clock, which suggests he pictures time running up and down. Because the two men's languages frame time in different directions, Tyler cannot tell whether 'above' means earlier or later. However, Zhang seemed to follow Tyler's 'forwards or backwards', which fits relativism: language affects how they talk about time but does not stop them understanding another way.

Boroditsky (2001) supports this. She found English speakers mostly describe time horizontally while Mandarin speakers also use vertical terms, and Mandarin speakers answered time questions faster after seeing vertical spatial primes. This matches the exact difference between Tyler and Zhang, so the explanation has scientific backing. Other research, such as studies showing that describing a face in words can change how well it is later recognised, also suggests words shape perception and memory.

On the other hand, Boroditsky's study was a laboratory task measuring reaction times, so it may not reflect everyday conversations like an email. Determinism might be offered instead, but it is too strong here: Zhang can clearly grasp Tyler's meaning, and Tyler could quickly learn what 'above' means once it is explained. The confusion might also simply come from Zhang being new to English rather than from deep differences in thought.

Overall, linguistic relativism explains Tyler's confusion well, because the misunderstanding comes from different language habits for describing time, and Boroditsky's evidence matches the case closely, although the problem is about wording and could easily be resolved.

How the marks are earned

Level 3 (7-9): accurate definition and contrast (AO1), sustained use of Tyler's and Zhang's actual words (AO2), and evaluation using Boroditsky, limits of the evidence and the determinism alternative leading to a clear judgement (AO3).
