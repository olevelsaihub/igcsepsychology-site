Questionnaires and interviews · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Question 5 · part (whole) · 12 marks

*5 Schaffer and Emerson (1964) conducted interviews with the mothers of 37 infants. The interviews took place at four-weekly intervals, in their family home, over a period of 12 months. The interviews were unstructured and used open-ended questions to gather information about an infant’s reaction to physical contact with their mother. The behaviours the researchers focused on included: • how the infant reacted when cuddled on their mother’s knee whether the infant tried to get physical contact • • whether the infant avoided physical contact • the mother’s reaction to the infant’s contact behaviours. Mothers were encouraged to give full descriptions of their infant’s behaviours. Often mothers would provide demonstrations of the behaviours that they had highlighted previously, without being asked. Schaffer and Emerson (1964) found two groups of infants, which they named ‘Cuddlers’ and ‘Non-cuddlers’, with typical responses including ‘loves being handled’ or ‘restless until put back in cot’. Evaluate the use of unstructured interviews to investigate human behaviour. (12)

Worked explanation

  1. AO1: what an unstructured interview is and the data it gives.
  2. AO2: apply to Schaffer and Emerson's home interviews with mothers.
  3. AO3 strengths: rich data, follow-up questions, rapport.
  4. AO3 weaknesses: interpretation bias, low replicability, social desirability, time.
  5. Conclusion.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

An unstructured interview is a self-report method in which the researcher has topics to cover but no fixed list of questions, so it runs more like a conversation and new questions are asked in response to what the person says. It mainly uses open-ended questions and produces qualitative data. In Schaffer and Emerson's study the researchers interviewed 37 mothers in their own homes every four weeks for a year, using open questions about how their infants reacted to physical contact, and mothers were encouraged to describe the behaviour fully.

A strength is the rich, detailed qualitative data. Descriptions such as 'loves being handled' give a much fuller picture of an infant's behaviour than a tick box, which helped the researchers identify 'cuddlers' and 'non-cuddlers'. Because the interview is flexible, the researcher can follow up an unclear answer and ask what the mother meant, so misunderstandings are reduced and validity increases. Interviewing in the family home may also help the mothers relax and build rapport, so they are more open and honest, and some even demonstrated the behaviour, which adds evidence.

However, qualitative answers have to be interpreted, so there is a risk of researcher bias in how the mothers' descriptions were categorised. Because each interview is different, the method is hard to replicate exactly and answers are hard to compare between mothers, which lowers reliability. Mothers might also give socially desirable answers in a face-to-face interview, for example presenting their baby as more affectionate than it really is, which would make the conclusions less accurate. Unstructured interviews are also time-consuming, which is why the sample was small.

Overall, unstructured interviews are very useful for exploring sensitive or complex behaviour in depth, as in Schaffer and Emerson's study, but because the data are open to bias and difficult to compare, their findings are best checked against more structured or observational evidence.

Levels-marked; this answer shows knowledge, consistent application and evaluation with a reasoned conclusion, as the top level needs.
