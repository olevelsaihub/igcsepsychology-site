Experiments: lab, field and natural · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Question 5 · part (whole) · 12 marks

*5 Godden and Baddeley (1975) conducted a field experiment to test memory. They used lists of words that were learned and then recalled in either the same or different environments to learning. They used scuba divers as participants who learned and recalled the words either underwater or on dry land. These environments were familiar to the divers. The scuba divers were allocated into the following different conditions: • Words learned underwater, recalled underwater. • Words learned underwater, recalled on dry land. Words learned on dry land, recalled on dry land. • • Words learned on dry land, recalled underwater. The word lists were prerecorded and presented to the scuba divers twice when they were in their learning environment. The words were read at two-second intervals in the recording. Godden and Baddeley (1975) found that more words were recalled when the divers were in the same environment in which they had learned the words, compared to when learning and recall took place in different environments. Evaluate the use of field experiments to investigate human behaviour. (12)

Worked explanation

  1. AO1: define a field experiment (natural setting, IV manipulated, DV measured).
  2. AO2: apply to divers: familiar settings, environment as IV, words recalled as DV.
  3. AO3 strengths: ecological validity; cause and effect.
  4. AO3 weaknesses: extraneous variables underwater; replication; artificial task.
  5. Conclusion: useful but needs support from controlled research.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Field experiments are carried out in a real-world setting that is natural for the participants, but the researcher still deliberately changes an independent variable and measures a dependent variable. In Godden and Baddeley's study the setting was natural for the participants because the scuba divers were used to being underwater and on land, and the researchers manipulated the IV: whether the words were learned and recalled in the same or a different environment. The DV was how many words the divers recalled.

One strength of field experiments is ecological validity. Because the divers were in places they knew, their memory performance is more likely to reflect how memory works in everyday life than if they had been tested in a laboratory. A second strength is that the IV is still manipulated, so cause and effect can be suggested: the divers recalled more when the two environments matched, so context seems to cause better recall.

However, a field setting gives less control over extraneous variables. Underwater, things such as cold, currents, visibility and noise from equipment could not be kept the same for every diver, so these may have affected recall rather than the context itself, which lowers internal validity. The study is also harder to replicate exactly, so reliability is difficult to check. In addition, the task itself was artificial: divers do not normally learn lists of words read from a recording, so the setting was realistic but the behaviour was not, which weakens the claim of ecological validity.

Overall, field experiments are useful when researchers want to see how behaviour happens in real settings while still testing a cause, as Godden and Baddeley did. But because control is lower and tasks are often still artificial, their findings should be supported by laboratory research before firm conclusions are drawn.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked: knowledge of field experiments, application to Godden and Baddeley, and sustained evaluation with a justified conclusion, all present for the top level.

Experiments: lab, field and natural · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Question 5 · part (whole) · 12 marks

* Becker et al. (2002) conducted a natural experiment in Fiji to investigate the impact of television on eating behaviours. They assessed attitudes to eating in 1995, just as television was being introduced to the native population. They returned in 1998 to assess eating behaviours after three years of television exposure. 63 girls with a mean age of 17.3 years took part in 1995. A second sample of 65 girls with a mean age of 16.9 years took part in 1998. To score eating behaviours, the two samples of girls completed a standardised EAT-26 questionnaire. They also answered questions about television viewing. Their weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) were also recorded. Comparisons between 1995 and 1998 data indicated that, after exposure to western imagery on television, the reports of disordered eating such as self-induced vomiting to lose weight had increased. Evaluate the use of natural experiments to investigate human behaviour. (12)
