Gibson's direct theory of perception · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Question 26 · part (whole) · 9 marks

*26 Liz was on a train looking out of the window. Although she did not feel like she was moving, she knew the train was travelling very fast. Liz could see a church and although the features of the church building were blurred and unclear, she knew it was far away. She could also see fields and trees stretching into the distance. Liz became aware that the train was slowing down, even though she could not hear the sound of it slowing down. Liz stood up and began to gather her belongings, but when she looked through the window of the train, she saw that the station was smaller than the trees she could also see, so she sat back down. Assess how well the Direct Theory of Perception (Gibson, 1966) can explain how Liz was able to interpret her surroundings. (9)

Worked explanation

  1. AO1: outline direct perception, optic flow, texture gradient as an invariant, relative size.
  2. AO2: link optic flow to sensing speed, blur to distance, station-versus-trees to distance from the station.
  3. AO3: strength (visual cliff, explains real-world movement), weakness (ignores prior knowledge/illusions, reductionist).
  4. Judgement: explains most of Liz's experience but not all.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Gibson's (1966) direct theory says perception is bottom-up: the pattern of light reaching our eyes, the optic array, contains all the information we need, so we do not have to rely on past knowledge. When we move, optic flow occurs: nearby things rush past quickly while distant things drift slowly, which tells us our speed and direction. Invariants, for example texture gradient (detail becoming finer with distance) and relative size also give direct information about how far away things are.

This fits Liz well. Although she cannot feel the train moving, the speed at which the trees and fields stream across her visual field gives her optic flow information that the train is going fast, and when that flow slows she can tell the train is braking without hearing it. The church looks blurred and lacks detail, so texture gradient tells her it is far off. When she sees the station looking smaller than the trees, relative size tells her the station is still a long way away, so she sits down.

There is support for the idea that depth perception comes directly from the senses: in Gibson and Walk's (1960) visual cliff study, young babies refused to crawl onto the 'deep' side of a visual cliff, suggesting depth perception is at least partly innate rather than learnt. The theory also suits real, moving environments like a train journey, where information is rich. However, it is reductionist because it looks only at sensory input and ignores how emotion, motivation or expectation shape what we see. It also cannot explain visual illusions, where people misperceive because they apply prior knowledge, and Liz may partly use knowledge that churches and stations are normally large buildings to judge their distance.

Overall, Gibson's theory explains Liz's perception of speed and distance well because she is moving through a rich real-world scene, but it cannot fully account for her interpretation because some of her judgements probably also rely on what she already knows about familiar objects.

How the marks are earned

Level 3: accurate, thorough knowledge of optic flow and invariants (AO1); every idea tied to a detail of Liz's journey (AO2); balanced evaluation with evidence and a reasoned judgement (AO3).
